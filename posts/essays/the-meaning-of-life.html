The Meaning of Life

April 29, 2024

At that time I was worrying about the meaning of life. I think such worry is essential around the age of twenty. Middle school or high school may be possible too, but when adolescence overlaps with questions of life, one can wander too much. Around twenty, when one has become freer, one should spend enough time thinking without being crushed. Even if no answer appears, the fact that no answer appeared can be carried as a kind of answer.

Then I was active in Catholic life. I served in liturgy, sometimes as an altar server, and read the Bible diligently. I read Catholic books and Protestant books as well. People speak confidently about the afterlife, but nothing is certain. Heaven is defined through Scripture, yet Scripture is a set of books approved by the Church. What appears in books the Church did not approve for lack of evidence might be closer to truth.

What matters is that the Church accepts everything on the basis of the belief that the Holy Spirit guides it. Even if past decisions were made under that guidance, perhaps people chose more passively according to conscience, or more aggressively despite conscience and even despite the Spirit. My doubt began with the question of whether there could truly have been no one devoted to securing the worldly power of the church organization. But even if I read books like Jeung San Do’s Dojeon, nothing spoke with certainty beyond “someone said so” or “a tradition says so.” Still, I did not conclude that because the world contains suffering, God does not exist. Like Tolstoy, I was vaguely thinking that the moment when one sees God’s face may not mean a global event like the rapture, but each person’s own death.

Only now do I realize that this was the thought I was having. At the time it was muddy and uncertain, a single idea. Yet people often say, when speaking of personal death, that we do not know when that day will come.

I was devout enough to ask for confession on the spot while walking with a priest. Then one day, after reaching the conclusion that none of it was certain, I thought I should take some distance. Without telling anyone, I stopped going to church and stopped all Catholic student activities. I closed the Bible and only read related books in the library. I wrestled with thoughts popular then, such as whether the sacraments might simply be European customs received the way Korea observes seasonal rites and holidays, or claims like those from Age of Aquarius prophecy books and Zeitgeist.

The claim that Christianity is the sum of older religions was amusing but not shocking. I thought it could be explained as a kind of ideological evolution. Perhaps Rome could accept Christianity because it had passed through such stages. What I wanted more was a ground on which I could stand and laugh off any attack on Catholicism. I wanted Peter - the rock.

Peter was literally rock. If one stands on him, anything is possible. I murmured, as he did, “You are the Son of God.” In that instant everything changed. It was instantaneous. As the words “the body of Christ” become fully spoken - words that cannot exist in the flow of time without consuming even the fraction of a second needed to utter them - and as the flour wafer becomes the body of Jesus, so the confession of Peter, when it leaves the mouth as a complete word, made me know that no such explanation was necessary. That is faith. One believes. Faith does not require evidence. Even when one trusts a swindler, one thinks there is evidence, but in fact the belief turns anything into evidence. The stock market is full of this. If one seeks evidence in faith, one can never believe. If one believes, everything becomes evidence of faith.

Giving money to a swindler is bad because one loses money and time. Not simply loses them, but hands them over and becomes weaker. Faith is not meant to weaken me. It strengthens me.

During that period of worry I read many books: religious books, popular science, economics. I must have been looking for a way to make life solid. I wanted to lean on faith, but my life felt too soft to stand even with a foundation. I searched for something that could build from root to pillar. In the end, the answer was that I had to live my life, and I had to build my faith. Even if Peter laid a firm foundation, raising the trunk was my work. I found no place on which to lean my life. There is no such thing. Life cannot be propped up on something else. I must raise it myself. That is living. All I need is the basic conviction that the whole of life will not collapse. That may be religion, or family, or something else.

Only then did I learn that not every answer can be found in life, and that the mere fact of having tried to find one can give great courage.

After that, I repeatedly returned to fervent faith and then stayed far from church. I tried reciting prayers daily, devoted myself to the rosary, read Scripture every day for a time, never missed the Angelus at another time, read The Imitation of Christ, went daily to Eucharistic adoration, and even prayed the Liturgy of the Hours every night without missing a day. My conclusion is that if a method does not fit me, there is nothing to be done. But every second of those fervent periods keeps me from losing faith. The loose periods are not times of losing faith and collapsing. The fervent periods harden the cells of my life. My life stands upright. Not having certainty about life is no longer a problem. All I needed was certainty that not having certainty about life does not mean life has no meaning.

I am still experimenting with my life. That experiment may continue until death, or I may find an answer before then and live with a peaceful mind until God’s kingdom comes. I am no longer curious about which will happen. Whatever happens, my life is not soft and shapeless.

Korean original: 인생의 의미
