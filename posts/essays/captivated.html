Captivated

July 2, 2026

한국어 원문 →

I was on a bus, happily speeding along the West Coast Expressway. When the map showed that we were not far from the Seohae Bridge, a factory caught my eye. The sun was about to set, and it was the time of afternoon when the vivid, lemon-flavored yellow of the sunlight begins to take on a reddish tint. In fact, with the trees and mountains there, I had never imagined there would be a factory in that spot. But the smoke coming from a chimney caught my gaze, and as I moved my eyes around to find out what that smoke was, I realized there was a factory there.

The smoke looked fixed in midair, like a ladder reaching up to the sky. Was the air too cold and heavy for it to spread out as soon as it left the chimney? For several dozen seconds, until I could no longer keep looking without turning my head, the smoke stayed in place with almost no change in shape. The sun behind it lit up the smoke alone, turning it into an unreal image, like an acrylic light suspended in the air.

Unreality.

Was that the only reason the smoke held my gaze? No matter how fascinating something is, is it possible to fall in an instant into the same state I used to enter in elementary school, watching ants move without noticing time pass?

I thought it was simply because it had been so long since I had seen such a fascinating sight, but that was not it. What could be so fascinating about sunlight scattering through smoke? Might there have been something hypnotic in that scene, something that stopped thought and fixed my gaze before I could think how fascinating it was, perhaps even before I could think rationally at all?

A sight that, in itself, will not let me simply look on calmly—the feeling I had when, from the summit of a low mountain, I unexpectedly saw a building I had never imagined would be there.

The smoke was not beautiful, but the sunlight was beautiful as always. Smoke itself may have no color, but the sunlight that pierced it and became a halo was yellow. And though the smoke was undoubtedly rising into the sky, the way it seemed to stay still gave me the illusion that time had stopped, even though the bus carrying me was clearly moving.

There are usually two ways something catches the eye. One is when I find it fascinating or am curious about it, decide I should look longer, and keep watching. The second is when my gaze is literally captured. A state of concentration in which all rational thoughts and memories are left behind, responding to an urgent signal from the optic nerve connected directly to the frontal lobe. Is a sight beautiful enough to stop the heart such an emergency that it shuts out bodily conditions and the workings of reason? Or is there something instinctive in it, as when seeing a lion right in front of you makes your heart race with fear and puts every nerve on alert?

Being captivated, smoke, sunlight. That scene from that day still gives me much to think about. The reason is that accounts of religious experiences, and even near-death experiences, consist mostly of visual information. If this feeling of being captivated grows more intense, would it not be natural to feel awe as well? And if you cannot look away and have no choice but to take in those images, you might also feel as though you have become smaller. So these thoughts keep returning to a single question.

What if, when I saw that smoke that day and moved my eyes around to find where it was coming from, what I found had been some religious site, rather than a factory and its chimney?
