An Outing to Jamsil Saenae

April 28, 2024

Around lunchtime today I went out toward Jamsil because I had something to do. The errand ended quickly, and the weather felt properly springlike, so I could walk around in a good mood. The sky was intensely blue, with scattered clouds drifting by. The sunlight was so bright that the contrast between shadow and light was dazzling, and the temperature was warm enough that I saw more people in short sleeves than at any time this year. I too was wearing a short-sleeved shirt and carrying a bag.

After getting off at Jamsil Saenae Station, I followed my companion through an exit so winding that I would not have been able to find my way out alone. The final exit led through a narrow underground passage that seemed to have been built grudgingly after complaints demanding an exit - too narrow even for stalls, let alone an underground shopping area. We walked through the pleasant street, met the rest of the group in front of McDonald’s, went into a back alley, and bought coffee from the cafe Hard Hand, which I had only heard of before. I did not order anything because I had drunk a venti Americano from Starbucks just before leaving, thanks to a coupon.

The work took only an hour. During that time I searched, half out of curiosity and half as window shopping, for devices that might be useful as writing tools. I did not find much. Pomera seems expensive because it has many functions for writing Japanese, but for someone who writes Korean, paying that price means paying for features I will never use. Even with the weak yen it needed about 400,000 won. Then I found a product called Neo by WordSmart. It seems discontinued, and at about 160,000 won it caught my interest, but I could not find any information that it supports languages other than English. Since the Freewrite Alpha looks similar and was aimed at users who were told there were no plans for non-English support, perhaps Neo was made for a similar audience. As for manual or electric typewriters that print on paper, the follow-up work after writing is too complicated, so I gave up.

The two-row Corona models look tempting, but they are English typewriters and I would have no use for them. Korean typewriters have late-era designs I do not much like, and after writing I would have to scan the pages and run character recognition. These days, if I take a photo with my phone and long-press the image, it can copy text. I tested that by email, and handwriting actually worked better than scans. I do not know how typewritten Korean would fare, but if I had to do that page by page every time I wrote, it would be something to consider only if writing became my profession. For now, no.

After the errand, I walked again through a street that felt like Seoul. I do not know exactly what makes a street feel Seoul-like to me, but Itaewon, City Hall, Jongno, and Hongdae do, while oddly places like Gyodae, Gasan Digital Complex, and Chungjeongno do not. It is only a purely personal feeling, and I do not know the criteria. In any case, the area near Jamsil Saenae Station felt richly like Seoul. Feeling that mood, I entered an Aladin used bookstore.

It felt like a warehouse of books. The shelves were packed tightly. The space itself was not small, but there were so many books that the aisles were quite narrow - just wide enough for two people to pass. Walking through it, I understood what a place with many books means. When I order used books online from Aladin, sometimes two of them come from Jamsil. Seeing this density, that made sense. I had received a text saying volume one of Hard-Boiled Wonderland and the End of the World had arrived at home, and since I had accidentally read volume two first, I should have been thinking only of that book. But with a field of books spread in front of me, I could not help opening whatever caught my curiosity.

I soon had a book about books and a book about experiences in Italy in my hands, and I kept checking this book and that. I did not read them fully; I was only confirming what books I had heard of were actually like. Then I saw Paul Auster’s books and was surprised that there were fewer copies than expected. Mr. Vertigo was a book I had considered buying new because there was no copy near where I live, so I opened it happily. But the previous owner had not trained the spine gently and seemed to have bent it harshly. When I opened the cover, a certain page opened by itself. I opened it as I had opened the books before it. Since this is literature printed and sold as a published work, I will not hide the line. What immediately entered my eyes was a curse-laden quotation. What drew my eyes even more was that the two quoted lines had been underlined in blue marker.

It was so absurd that I took a picture. Later in the book those markings changed to highlighter. The back part was very clean, so I could not even tell whether anyone had read it. Perhaps the owner studied fiction that way. I do not know the intention, but I cannot understand why such a book was not caught during inspection and was being sold. They certainly check condition when buying used books.

Strangely, the moment I saw those underlines, the pleasure vanished, as if I had found an insect in food. I checked only the books I already held, paid, and left.

I read several Paul Auster books after Moon Palace in college because its strange sense of speed stayed with me, and I had meant to read Mr. Vertigo too, since it is known as a major work. I do not know when I will read it now. Probably not for a while.

That was today’s Jamsil outing. I went to look for Haeju Naengmyeon because it was nearby, but it seems they made enough money to build a new building toward Jamsil Stadium. I did not get to taste it, but even if I had gone, I would not have ordered the signature spicy naengmyeon and would have chosen the mild version, so I am not too disappointed. While searching for the location, I learned there is a meal kit. I may buy one and try it soon.

When I got back onto the outer-circle Line 2 train, the outing was completely over. What remained in memory was that surprisingly few people got on and off at Samseong and Gyodae Stations.

Korean original: 잠실 새내 나들이
