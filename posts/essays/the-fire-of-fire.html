The Fire of Fire

October 9, 2026

한국어 원문 →

I write “불.”

Looking at what I have written, I find the meaning unclear, so I add “fire” beside it. This written character that entered my mind did not come into being out of empty space. Nothing comes to mind out of nowhere. It flows in from somewhere and takes shape. I wrote “불 fire,” and that was the process by which something that had come to mind acquired a physical substance. Insofar as anyone who sees these characters thinks of fire, fire itself, a drawing of flames, and the written word for fire are no different to human beings. I can write the word for fire, but I do not learn that word by looking at fire. No one does. It is impossible. We learn the concept of fire within our culture, and its name is simply part of that learning. The reason we look at fire and call it fire is that the sight of something burning resembles the image that the written word “fire” points to in our minds. The image that “fire” points to may differ from person to person, but it is similar enough for us to think of the same thing as fire. So fire in a novel is accepted as fire because it uses the written word directly to point to the fire in our minds, while fire in a film may not be fire if it cannot point to the “real fire” in our minds.

That fire, inherited from culture, seeped into me through all kinds of stories. The more fundamental something is, the more it is simply self-evident, rather than something that can be made up of specific explanations. The Pythagorean theorem, too, exists in every triangle in the world because it is so self-evident. Extracting only what they have in common and expressing it as a formula is, if anything, unnatural. Fire is in a lighter, in news of wildfires, and inside a car engine. When I lose my reason and wander, what is inside me is, of course, fire too. Where was this concept before it seeped into me? The interval between the moment I write the word for fire and the first moment the concept of fire seeped into me no longer matters. I cannot escape it anyway. But before it seeped into me? My parents, my parents’ parents, and the generations of their parents must each have had their own concept of fire. And they, too, must have spoken and written a word meaning fire. And. Before that…?

Things I have seen in history books pass through my mind. A lamp lighting a Buddhist shrine, a lamp a scholar has lit to read by, candlelight burning far away in Europe, a bed of charcoal laid in a forge to heat iron, and, before that, a kitchen where precious embers were carefully tended, and torches.

Then, suddenly, a moment comes to mind. Was that the first moment? A burning branch someone brought from the forest, and the first sound they must have made while pointing at that fire: “Au.” That startled sound, “au,” pointed to fire. The sound symbolizes the specific appearance of that fire that pierced through that person. It is my word “fire,” which turns countless appearances into a concept. A word standing at precisely the opposite end of time.

Did all the concepts come into being in a single instant when that person said “au”? Since fire exists, might its concept, too, have always been drifting somewhere, even before people noticed it? We give babies names and call them young people now, but might it have been decided from the very beginning that they would be born now?

I try saying “불,” and then, a little absurdly, “fire.”

As though an answer might come from the beginning of the universe, and as though that moment when the first combustion appeared in the universe, when stars began to form, might turn toward me and respond.
