Jongno Streets in Spring

April 28, 2024

In spring, merely walking quietly makes many thoughts leap out. First comes the simple thought that spring air feels good. Then memories of good things from the past, and regrets about what my life might have become if an unexpected variable had appeared in the middle and things had not flowed as they did, suddenly begin linking tail to tail in a way they would not have on the same walk in winter.

One day, while walking in Jamsil, I suddenly remembered the time I walked along Jongno. I was walking in the spring of 2024, but the memory that appeared came from twenty years earlier, in 2004. Seoul still had not fully escaped the fever of cheering from the World Cup. The press was struggling to keep alive the newly coined phrase “plaza culture,” and seeing foreigners on the street had suddenly become normal. It was a university summer vacation, and I was going back and forth to Jongno every day for an English academy. Jongno 2-ga, full of bars, was very quiet when we came out for lunch, except for a few restaurants that served meals. I do not know where all the office workers went for lunch, but those taking the same class as I did always went to a doenjang-jjigae place behind the academy. Since we could not eat the same thing every day, we ordered the same menu together and chose a different shared menu the next day, eventually trying everything.

Sometimes we ordered Hansot lunch boxes. After eating, others drank coffee, but I still did not understand why people drank coffee. Aside from drinking a can of Let’s Be at eleven at night to stay up during exam periods, I did not even drink vending-machine coffee. During that time I wandered outside the academy. If it rained, I had no choice but to stay in the classroom; otherwise I walked the Jongno streets in the sunlight.

Had the Cheonggyecheon overpass already been removed? I do not know. I did not go all the way to Cheonggyecheon. I only took a simple route along the streets between Jongno and Cheonggyecheon, as far as Jonggak, then returned along Jongno. Even then I looked around blankly. The broad-blocked street was meant for many people, but because it was midday and not dark, there were few people - a paradoxical scene. The sunlight was heating the asphalt. Buildings were everywhere, but most shops were closed. The air was warm, the street was quiet, and I was walking in the middle of it. It looked almost like a street in a zombie film. Then, when I reached Jongno, cars suddenly filled the road and people seemed to pour out of Jonggak Station, walking busily in every direction. The contrast gave me an inexplicable pleasure.

It would have been nice to cross Jongno and look around Insa-dong, but the contrast between those two streets alone was enough. Whether it was worth remembering did not matter. What mattered was that I was there and felt it with my whole body. Looking back now, I think such accumulated sensations have had many invisible effects on who I am. They were not things I accomplished by thinking. They were situations that came to me and touched me on their own, like radiation.

The street at lunchtime was warm, but the same street after class ended felt different. The temperature was still warm, but rather than the soft warmth of lunch, it was a tired, languid warmth, like lying in a bathtub while traveling. It seemed to contain more exhaust and dust. Most of all, as evening came, people leaving work, people eating after work, and people drinking after work mixed together, and the wide street became full of people moving back and forth. At lunch I had felt the street with my whole body; in the evening I was busy avoiding people. Everything felt like stimuli. If I had gone only in the evening, that would have seemed natural. But because I had walked there at lunch, it became unnatural.

The flashing neon signs felt strange, and the music from speakers at karaoke entrances and bar windows seemed to shout that the shady asphalt of lunchtime had become dark asphalt pounding with colorful sound. Countless people flowed like water, while many others stood in groups like rocks planted in a stream, ignoring the current. A little later, before night had even fully arrived, drunk people shouting slurred Korean began to appear.

But that too was spring. In winter, such scenes do not look leisurely. Even there, once people choose a destination, the cold makes them hurry. In spring - and in summer too - the warmth gives the street a kind of leisure.

When I passed people shouting after drinking, I frowned because the noise hurt my ears. But I walked that way almost every day because although I disliked those stimuli, the scene itself was enjoyable. If I had gone straight down the main Jongno road and entered Jongno 3-ga Station, I would not have seen it. Even now, when I visit the area, I always go through that middle street. Even when I walk along Cheonggyecheon, from Jongno 3-ga I turn back toward it. Places lively at night remain full of ease by day. People who come at night see which shops change, but people who pass only by day cannot tell. Even if more than half close in a recession, it would be hard to notice by day. That is what makes it a good place to feel memory again.

People who have known Jongno for a long time would say it had already changed a lot even in 2004. But someone who first saw it then can feel, in 2024, that it has not changed much, and go home comforted.

Korean original: 봄날의 종로거리
