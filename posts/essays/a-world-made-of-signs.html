A World Made of Signs

April 30, 2024

In a university literature course I learned the concept of “defamiliarization.” It was probably about literature’s useful power to breathe freshness into what we pass by every day and help us see it from a new angle. At the time I thought, “This must be a translated concept from abroad, but why translate it so carelessly?” Yet perhaps there is no need to express an easy idea in difficult words. It becomes embarrassing only when the term itself is used as a test of whether one knows the concept.

I feel resistance when people sanctify the exact words used to point at something and try not to change a single particle. “Defamiliarization” is fine when used with an explanation, but using it as a technical term to make things easier for someone who has never heard it - someone like a version of me who never took a liberal arts literature class - is wrong enough. Still, for now I have no better word. I want to express the feeling that one’s surroundings become a background that is no longer everyday but something strange. It happens in the middle of waking, when I move from realizing where I am to realizing who I am, then outward again to the place and time I occupy, and information forces itself in.

On the subway to work, I lowered my head and dozed. As I woke, I was imagining writing something in Korean on a manual typewriter, though the next thought covered it so I cannot know the content. It was not quite a dream; half asleep, feeling the weight of my phone in my hand and hearing the station announcement, I imagined it. Then I thought I should check what station the train was stopping at and opened my eyes. What first entered my eyes was the opposite seat with two empty spaces, the window above it, and the scenery beyond.

The view was full of trees. I had expected to see only sky, so trees were unexpected. Since spring was almost becoming summer, they were fully green. I slowly thought that the trees were not green as a single thing; countless tiny green leaves attached to them made them appear that color. When I closed my eyes again, I imagined countless little words saying “green leaf” hanging on every tree. Opening my eyes, I felt as if such labels could be attached to the visible trees.

Even if the name of a tree were engraved in every cell of the tree, it would not be so different from seeing the patterns of cell walls. Whether “leaf” is written on a leaf, or a leaf pattern is read as leaf, there is not much difference if everyone has agreed to read it that way. If someone came with the old idea that there should be a Chinese character for every thing in the world, no one could object completely. If it becomes agreed, it will be used; if not, it will not.

Imagining leaves turning into letters made me understand that even the same phenomenon can become strange when expressed in writing. I do not know how to do it. But I know it can be done. It is like not knowing how to build a car but knowing how to ride one to Busan; like knowing how to use something without knowing why; like not being able to draw or make something but still being able to appreciate it.

I imagined a scene seen from the sky. Instead of wind, the heavy sound of classical horns lay in the air. From gray letters spelling “cloud,” countless transparent letters spelling “water” poured down. Through the middle passed a large word, “subway,” wrapped in letters saying “steel plate.” Beneath it ran two rails made of countless small letters spelling “rail.” Along the rails, the large word “gravel bed” repeated, and beside it stood a gray “sound barrier.” Outside that were brown “trees,” and on every stroke of the word “tree” were many green “leaf” letters, fluffy like the fur of a long-haired dog.

Isn’t the world inside writing like this? A world made of writing, where everything is letters. Perhaps that is why when we open a book, everything is black letters on white paper. And just as our world, made of atoms and empty space, appears full of varied things to us who live inside it, if we enter that written world the letters too become substance and fill the space around us. Then meaning becomes central, and the spaces between strokes no longer matter.

Reading and writing are not merely acts of using signs or language. They connect a new world with ours. Reading is putting one’s head inside a world made of letters and trying to experience how it looks from within. Writing is converting a new world into letters so that it can be carried. When we bring that converted world and unfold it, we can look inside and feel it again.

Korean original: 기호로 된 세상
