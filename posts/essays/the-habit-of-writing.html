The Habit of Writing

May 1, 2024

Writing is a habit. Conversely, writing that has not become habitual is not writing but composition. This may be my own usage, so I should explain. Writing is the everyday act of writing: not sweating over a subject, but writing because it is written, writing because it comes. Composition is making a subject and constructing a piece to fit it. If writing is pulling thread out from a ball of yarn, composition is making clothing from that thread. Composition requires denser labor and technique. Writing has honesty, but may lack polish. As you can see, this is circular logic.

It is not difficult. I call it writing because one writes, and composition because one builds a piece with effort toward a purpose. Perhaps others do not distinguish them. A writer’s published work is composed; a casual blog post about daily life may be written.

I am talking about writing: not making a structured piece with a special purpose and outline, but writing thoughts as they come. I try to do that. If writing requires the effort of composition, it cannot last. I go to the gym, but not more than forty minutes. If I exercise until I am exhausted, my weak will keeps me away for a month or two. If I exercise only thirty or forty minutes, excluding warm-up and shower, I can go every day. Writing is the same. Sometimes I may compose fiction that follows a deliberate outline with force. But ordinary writing must not take force.

Writing requires writing a lot, comparing one’s own texts, and trying changes. For that, there must first be plenty of text. “Not well written” is something one can say only after putting a piece before someone, even if that someone is oneself. Without putting it into the form of writing, one cannot say one cannot write well. The less one shows, the easier it becomes to be satisfied with oneself.

I have heard that Instagram and other media cause deprivation and inferiority. That depends on sensitivity, but they also allow some self-objectification. Someone who thinks he has exercised quite a bit can compare himself to people who exercise seriously without entering a contest. What once had to be asked through another’s eyes is now before us in countless samples, if only we are honest.

If one wants to go beyond the current stage and train hard, help from others is natural. But more importantly, comparison prevents the arrogance of doing nothing and saying, “I might already be enough.”

Writing is the same. There are countless readable texts. If I have enough of my own writing, comparison is not hard. If asked whether my writing is good, bad, or average, I would say perhaps it barely reaches average; I would not say, “I have not written much, but if I tried seriously, I would write well.” More samples give more grounds for objectivity.

One more thing: I think writing can be a habit, but composition cannot. For me, writing resembles the occupational disease Amanda has in the film The Holiday, where she automatically hears advertising copy for every situation. When I read one novelist for a long time, I begin describing my current situation in the narrator’s tone, or in the translator’s tone if it is translated fiction. Similarly, when I repeatedly unfold thoughts into writing, I begin wanting to write situations in my own writing voice. Passing through that gate lets thought branch and grow. If I merely try to write vaguely, I cannot move one step beyond the first thought.

I have not yet arrived, but as these experiences accumulate, I believe I will discover my own style.

Above all, writing is personal. It proceeds with thought, like conversation, but as a human being’s private act. Composition is different: it speaks with a larger voice, not to the person in front of you. What matters is that even a personal act must be repeated to become available when needed. If I go a month without talking to anyone because I am busy, my speech becomes awkward. Others may not notice, but I feel something unnatural. Speech recovers quickly when used, but awkwardness in writing does not disappear so quickly. When I wrote by hand in notebooks, such periods produced only one- or two-line notes. It would be the same now. It is good when writing grows longer as one writes. For that, nothing seems more important than writing every day in a way that can lengthen naturally.

What I meant to say is this: writing should become a habit; composition should not. In technical work or exercise, people say most often, “Relax.” It is enjoyable to write every day and observe my words gradually becoming easier for others to understand. But planning, outlining, assigning paragraphs, and forcing turns of direction every day as if composing an important piece is impossible. Such pieces should be composed only at important moments, and above all should not be forced on oneself daily.

This may sound like advice, but I am not in a position to advise anyone, especially about writing, which is only my hobby. It is more of an excuse for myself: I try to relax in writing, write every day even if the text is neither long nor grand, do not try to produce a perfectly built piece every day, avoid serialization because promise itself becomes pressure, and am simply explaining why.

Korean original: 글쓰기 습관
