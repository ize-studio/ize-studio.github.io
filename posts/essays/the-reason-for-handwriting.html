The Reason for Handwriting

There was a reason why, for several years, I wrote by hand in notebooks whenever I jotted things down. It has only been a few years since I began using my phone or computer files in earnest. At most, about three years. Before that, I had run a blog too, but that was separate from what I wrote by hand. It was writing meant to be shown to others, mostly informational writing, and therefore relatively “less valuable” as something to record by hand in a notebook. Value is determined by use, so I mean less valuable to me. Useful writing increases in value when it is made public. I could say I used the blog as a stall for information, like a flea market where one puts out objects one does not need. But even before now, I could have saved things as Word files. The reason I still insisted on handwriting was, unexpectedly, that when I wrote with a keyboard, my writing speed was too fast.

Writing is made of two actions carried out in parallel. One is observing the thoughts unfolding in my head while leaving them freely spread out. The other is writing down and looking at the sentences made through that process. To distinguish more precisely: separating the thought, observing it and making it into a sentence, writing that sentence down, and finally looking at what has been written while continuing to connect and choose the next thought. These four things proceed while constantly checking one another. When a thought branches out, the writing follows. If I decide to subdivide and explain a sentence, a sentence that explains the previous sentence is made in the writing as well. This is a kind of real-time feedback action. Once a sentence begins, it continues in real time, like a bicycle chain where the front part moves to the back and draws the front out again, and then the back follows, repeating without rest until the thought is cut off. When that happens, I enter a state in which I do not have to struggle to write, but can simply take down the flow of thought naturally.

When I first tried writing on a computer, it did not work, and without knowing why, I used notebooks. I have always liked stationery, so whenever I found a thick notebook that looked comfortable for writing with a fountain pen, I bought it, and I never ran out of notebooks. Then one day I had reason to look through an old notebook, and I could not tell which part was where. Since it was offline paper material that could not be searched, I had no choice but to read the hard-to-read handwriting from the beginning, one character at a time. In the end, I gave up before reading even half of it. That became the occasion for thinking about why I could not write on a computer. I reread one by one the blog posts I had written and rarely looked at again because I had no need for them, and in the end I found the answer.

Looking at the blog posts, I saw that they would state only what was necessary and then suddenly stop. For people who had come to find the information they needed, once they had read that information, they probably did not care how the post ended. But the problem was that even in writing about thoughts, there was a feeling that the writing suddenly broke off at some point. Until then, I had never looked closely at myself writing, so I could not know. But when I observed with self-awareness, it turned out to be a surprisingly simple problem. In short: the speed of thought could not keep up with the speed of writing. When I wrote by hand, thought was very free, like a multiverse unfolding and then meeting our time in the present, merging into one and being fixed as a single present. While I wrote, thoughts that branched wildly became a sentence only at the moment that sentence was written. If one writes too slowly, one only sinks blankly into thought. But if the speed of writing is faster than the speed at which thought freely spreads, one has to wait until the thought reaches that sentence and begins moving ahead of it. In my case, the former was possible, but the latter was not. There were times when I wrote so slowly that my mind wandered, but there were no times when I wrote so fast that I could wait. The moment the speed of writing became faster, the moment I stopped writing, the thought for that writing stopped together with it. I could no longer write that piece. So it was not the act of writing by hand itself that had been necessary. What had been necessary was not disturbing thought, and by chance handwriting had the right speed.

From then on, I began looking for mechanical keyboards. Not just mechanical keyboards, but keyboards with round keys that imitated old typewriters. The reason was simple. Keyboards with round keys have wide spaces between keys, and because the keys are round, there is also space above and below them, so the places without keys are completely open gaps. If a finger slips into one of those places, it takes time to find its position again. The trick is to press slowly and deliberately. Then the writing speed inevitably slows down. To me, they were both pretty and customized for my writing. At work I had to type fast no matter what, so I used an ordinary keyboard, but at home I used that kind of keyboard.

Writing now on an e-reader that uses e-ink is the same. In the case of an e-ink screen, the disadvantage is that screen updates are slow when writing characters. But when I write personal pieces, my speed is not that fast. I do not write at a speed where I would feel that characters appear slowly, so it is not a problem. No, I do not feel the problem; for me, it is not a problem.

Now that things have turned out this way, notebooks have become neglected burdens. From time to time, I set aside time to move past notebooks into files, and it is incredibly tedious. Sometimes I find a treasure that makes me think, “I had this thought?” But most of it is more or less the same, because it is my own thought. Even so, if I am going to preserve it anyway, I know that files are far more useful. It is not that I intend to throw away or shred the notebooks. I simply think I need file versions too.

Writing freely without rushing is possible only when thought is allowed to be free. A writing tool should be chosen above all from the perspective of whether it lets thought be free, and unfortunately, whether it suits that purpose has to be tried one by one. Everyone is different. I solved it simply through speed control, but if someone finds that every thought disappears the moment they place their fingers on a keyboard, even after being reborn, then there is nothing to be done. There may be people who write better on the QWERTY keyboard of a phone. I understand all of it. Even if I hear some Mozart-level genius story about someone who perfectly organizes the whole piece in their head beforehand and then types it out quickly while looking at it, I would simply think, “I see.” People outside me are other people, so everything is possible. I only have to be faithful to my own thoughts and my own writing.
