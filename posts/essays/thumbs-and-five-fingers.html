Thumbs and Five Fingers

April 30, 2024

Before the entire front of a phone became a screen, entering anything required a keypad, so I had no choice but to use the Cheonjiin keyboard. My phone used Cheonjiin, but each manufacturer had its own system for combining Korean consonants and vowels within the number pad. Very early PCS phones sometimes forced the cursor to move left to right while selecting consonants and vowels, but as mobile phones spread explosively, these clever systems appeared. Once a system became familiar in the hands, it became difficult to change to another maker, because writing with both thumbs made the habit strong. That too must have been part of the manufacturers’ strategy.

Touch screens appeared around the same time mobile internet browsers became serious. I vividly remember using Twitter on my first touch-screen phone. Back then I even calculated page size because of data speed. I searched for and used a mobile browser that did not load graphics on Twitter. When the keypad disappeared, typing without looking became impossible. And because the screen had to be pressed with a fingernail rather than touched, the input situation changed completely.

To avoid typos, I had to look at the on-screen keyboard. English had already become too inconvenient with the old repeated-key method, so I had changed to QWERTY; it was only a matter of time before I used QWERTY for Korean too.

That moment came after I switched to an iPhone. Before that, I used an LG Argo phone. Because fingernail tapping felt inaccurate, I used the stylus that came in the box. With a single stylus, writing Korean on Cheonjiin was much easier than moving around a QWERTY layout.

After switching to the iPhone, the capacitive screen responded to touch, so typing with thumbs was not bad. At first typos were inevitable, but after I became used to it, they dropped to the level I had with Cheonjiin. I had thought that because I write slowly, I make fewer typos than others. Later I was surprised to find people around me who typed much faster and made fewer mistakes.

For me, writing with two thumbs on a QWERTY keyboard and writing with all ten fingers on a Bluetooth keyboard are not very different in speed. I do not type especially fast on the touch keyboard, nor have I tried to raise my words per minute on a physical keyboard. It is only a tool. Still, because I like tools, I own several keyboards.

The difference is not the thumbs themselves, but the touch keyboard. On a screen, if I do not keep paying attention, typos appear, so my gaze must stay on the keyboard. Since I do not type fast, this prevents mistakes well, but it disperses attention. When I begin with something already to say, it is fine. But for writing that follows the flow of thought, it interferes. I sometimes have to write deliberately more slowly. Strangely, as I slow down to prevent typos, thought seems to slow as well because I am attending to touch position. However perfectly the two are coordinated, it is hard to call that natural writing.

I often write on my phone. When a subject occurs to me, even if I do not write an essay, I at least make a note of the topic. If the thought branches, I note some of that too, but beyond a certain point I hold the phone properly with both hands and focus. Thinking about how to reduce attention, I realized that the problem was typos, and since I had to look at the keyboard anyway, making it larger would make it easier to see and reduce errors. My phone keyboard is maximized left and right and stretched vertically to prevent mistaken taps above or below. Because my fingers already move only at a speed my eyes can follow, the effect has been considerable.

The only way to avoid this whole worry is a physical keyboard. To put down a physical keyboard and place both hands on it, a subway is not ideal; a cafe or table is better. Then fingers other than the thumbs enter through touch. All ten fingers print letters, my eyes remain on the screen, and the newly typed letters combine with the existing text to follow the flow of thought. To others I may not look fast, but I do not stop; finger by finger, little movements fill the screen with black letters. From the screen alone, thumb writing may look similar. But in reality, the participating fingers are different.

My conscious mind’s task is to minimize the difference. The unconscious must keep growing branches of thought. The writing environment cannot always be the same. Different tools and settings may reduce efficiency or create an unfamiliar atmosphere, but consciousness must gather those conditions and make them feel like a good environment. Or, if it cannot make every setting good, it must at least make the atmosphere similar.

Then, whatever the middle process, I think the writing that emerges can become similar. Writing on the subway or bus may inevitably become lighter in tone, but if that is the only difference, it is not bad.

I think writing is something one grows. A thought sprouts from a seed, and once I confirm the sprout, I plant it in the writing so the sprout and the text grow together. It does not appear automatically by merely waving fingers, and worrying does not improve it. Giving more fertilizer or water does not instantly make a plant larger or a crop better. If a farmer does the same thing every year and the result changes wildly depending on the brand of plastic used in a greenhouse, everyone would say something is strange. If sunlight and rainfall cause small variations but the quality and quantity are mostly consistent, people say the farm is worth trading with.

I wish my writing were like that. I do not want the mood or length to swing wildly because of secondary things like writing tools. Repeated experiment and practice will make that happen.

Korean original: 엄지와 다섯 손가락
