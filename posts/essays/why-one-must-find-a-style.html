Why One Must Find a Style

April 27, 2024

The first word always has impact. For a reader it may be nothing more than a trail marker at the start of a mountain path, but for the writer it is like the gunshot at the starting line. Before the first word, the body is tense as rock; then, in the next instant, it sprints. It is a symbolic moment on the boundary. People say one must relax whatever one does, and writing is the same. If the beginning already contains too much force, the writer cannot overcome that tension, squeezes out sentences, and at some unexpected moment has no choice but to end the piece. That happens when the impact of the first word is accepted as impact. It is the starting point, yes, but it should be thought of differently. A marble is held at the top of a sloping rail, and beginning a piece of writing is merely removing the board that blocks it.

I should not run forward. I should let the sentence begin rolling by gravity like a marble, then follow its path. If I tense up there, the marble rolls alone while I fall behind and cannot catch up as it accelerates. When the marble reaches its destination, the writing ends regardless of whether I have managed to transfer it into words.

Even if I relax and follow, writing it down is another problem. Thought can flow in any direction, and it can rejoin the original current in an instant. Blind transcription may cut across and block that flow, or the writing itself may run ahead and change the direction of thought. If I begin without treating the first word’s pressure as pressure, I must keep going naturally and without force to the end. But then “naturally” becomes an obstacle again. How does one follow naturally? In dictation, writing what one hears is natural. But what if what must be dictated is an image or a moving scene? If it is imagined conversation, I can write it as it is. If it is an inner image, I cannot. I must make sentences. That is where the problem begins.

Think of studying English. Suppose that in conversation, I must hear the other person, analyze grammar, identify parts of speech, interpret, connect meanings, understand it in Korean, make a reply, and then compose that reply in English grammar. Written out like that, it sounds like something from a book called One Hundred Reasons You Do Not Need to Study English. At some level, one must react automatically and receive language as images. A reply too must come more than half reflexively before it can be called conversation. That is why conversation, unlike solving English test questions, requires practice.

Handling mental images while writing is the same. If this is a conversation with myself, and the mind gives me images and scenes, then I need to be able to write more than half reflexively. Otherwise, while I hesitate, the thought stops. Strictly speaking, writing may not be a battle against time, but in free writing it is a battle of timing. To minimize hesitation, I must write down such thoughts many times and build a store of relatively fixed expressions. By fixed I do not mean stamped out identically. I mean having a candidate set, like “for this kind of image, choose from this kind of word.” When typing Chinese characters on a computer, one writes the sound in Hangul, presses the Hanja key, sees a list of characters, and chooses the intended one.

In the same way, I connect several expressions to one image, as several Hanja are connected to one pronunciation. While transcribing thought, I only choose. When sentences flow with time, I must minimize the moment when only the sentence stops because I am asking, “What expression should I use?”

I am still practicing this. If a person has written enough, I think it is impossible not to have a style. In my case, I write slowly, and when a piece ended, I did not have to regret it. That probably did not help. Can one practice English conversation by talking to oneself endlessly? The practice of finding style began only after I started challenging myself to go as far as possible without breaking the flow.

The basic assumption that personal writing is only for myself must have been the greatest obstacle to developing style. It is true that thought is in my head and known only to me. But expressing something in writing, even if I destroy it immediately, is something beyond the purely personal. Like a knife or lighter, it creates something outside the body. If I create something anyway, basic courtesy toward what I make demands that I not make trash.

So I write a lot. No - even without that, I simply like writing. It is mysterious and beautiful when thoughts that were floating in the air take shape. For someone who writes, helping that beautiful thing exist each time, at the length it ought to have, should be a goal reached through one’s own effort.

Korean original: 문체를 찾아야 하는 이유
