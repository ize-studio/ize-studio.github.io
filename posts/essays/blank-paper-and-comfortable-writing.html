Blank Paper and Comfortable Writing

April 30, 2024

“Blank paper.”

There is an expression, a blank state. It means a state in which nothing comes to mind, or thought stops because of shock or embarrassment. The mind feels white like blank paper. Did people think that way because, long ago, they had to place a sheet of paper before them and write something on it?

“This paper is as white as my mind.”

Times have changed, and the place where we write is not necessarily white paper. A word processor shows an imitation of paper, lets us adjust margins, and displays what will be printed, but that does not make it paper. The feeling right before writing is probably similar whether one opens a memo app or a word processor.

Likewise, writing on a phone does not feel like facing blank paper. I also like writing on an e-reader, whose e-ink background is a pale olive or something like paper behind glass. All of these express paper prepared for writing. But because settings and colors vary, the pressure does not necessarily come as whiteness. Since I prepare to write only after something appears that I want to say, I do not open a window and think, “What should I do?” or “What should I write?” I do sometimes wonder what will happen if, with time, I become obligated to write and face that feeling. I do not know how I would see a blinking cursor then. I am not curious.

Because I dislike forcing things, imagining that pressure does not suit me.

I thought of this suddenly because I remembered the film Limitless. I watched it several times for its visual effects, but after learning that it was inspired by Adderall, I stopped watching much because those effects began to feel like drug glorification. There is a scene in which the protagonist, unable to write, takes the drug and writes like mad. Because the drug’s effect must be emphasized, the contrast is extreme. The writing scene, with graphics added, feels unreal. But the scene of not writing looks like a hidden camera placed before a truly uninspired writer: staring at a blinking cursor in a word processor, writing one line, erasing it with backspace, failing to withstand the angry blinking cursor, playing with a basketball, going out to buy alcohol.

Sometimes I stare at the screen like that when writing a report. In my head I am arranging the table of contents, but before my eyes is an empty document and a blinking cursor. I sometimes wonder what it would feel like if I were not arranging headings in my head, if I had no idea what to write. Reports are different from essays or fiction because their form and purpose are set. If a report cannot be written, either the details or the purpose are unclear.

But when free writing cannot be written, it only means thought has stopped. If I follow thought well, that does not happen. Sometimes the intended direction changes while writing. Some people say their personality requires them to build the entire skeleton first, but I cannot write that way. When one section ends and I must fill the next, thinking “how do I connect this?” cuts the thought. That is the state closest to blankness for me. The cursor blinks, but no direction forward is visible.

At that point the piece is over. I cannot write further. Since writing is also a tool for thought, I cannot say “in the end, only the text remains.” The thoughts born in the process of writing are more important than the completed text. Technically cutting into a piece merely to finish it feels poisonous to me. Reports do not feel that way, so this must apply mainly to writing done because I want to write. In situations where a finished text is required, I will probably adjust. When I say I like writing, I mean writing as a tool that frees thought. Once thought becomes free, blankness is no longer pressure.

It is like a wide field: to a general at war it is a heavy objective to seize and defend through countless battles; to a traveler it is simply pleasant land where one may go this way or that.

Korean original: 백지와 편안한 글쓰기
